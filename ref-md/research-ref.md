# Tourism Management and UGC Empirical Study Skill

Use this skill when the user wants to design, analyze, write, revise, or evaluate an empirical study related to:

* Tourism Management
* Online tourism platforms
* User-generated content, especially online reviews
* Hotels, restaurants, destinations, attractions, Airbnb, OTAs, or travel platforms
* Review volume, ratings, sentiment, helpfulness, length, readability, concreteness, topic, similarity, or textual quality
* Platform interventions, policy changes, feature launches, review display changes, ranking changes, badges, review summaries, incentives, verification systems, or other platform design changes
* Difference-in-differences, event study, panel fixed effects, cross-platform DID, PSM-DID, robustness checks, mechanism analysis, or heterogeneity analysis

This skill should not be limited to AI-related topics. Treat AI as only one possible platform intervention. The broader research angle is: how changes in tourism-platform information environments affect UGC production, UGC quality, consumer information, and business outcomes.
---
# Core Mission

When this skill is used, help the user produce work that resembles a rigorous empirical paper in Tourism Management / Information Systems / Marketing / Platform Economics.

The final output should help answer four central questions:

1. What is the empirical phenomenon?
2. What is the causal or quasi-causal identification strategy?
3. How does the focal change affect UGC quantity and quality?
4. What behavioral mechanisms explain the effect?

Always push the work beyond a simple correlation study. Prefer a structure with:

* Clear research context
* Clear treatment or shock
* Treated and control groups
* Pre/post comparison
* Rich UGC outcome variables
* Mechanism tests
* Heterogeneity analysis
* Robustness checks
* Theoretical and managerial implications

---

# Default Research Logic

Use the following logic as the default template:

A tourism platform, destination, or business environment changes users’ information environment or contribution incentives. This change affects how users generate UGC. The study empirically tests whether UGC changes, how it changes, why it changes, and for whom the effect is stronger.

General causal chain:

Platform / market change
→ user information environment or contribution motivation changes
→ UGC generation behavior changes
→ review quantity, valence, length, sentiment, content, concreteness, helpfulness, or similarity changes
→ implications for platforms, tourism businesses, and consumers

# Research Question Patterns

When helping formulate research questions, use one or more of the following patterns:

## Main Effect Questions

* Does the platform intervention affect subsequent UGC generation?
* Does the focal shock affect review volume, rating, sentiment, or review content?
* How does the platform feature change the quantity and quality of online reviews?
* Does the intervention alter users’ willingness to contribute reviews?

## Mechanism Questions

* Is the effect driven by substitution, learning, herding, differentiation, motivation crowding-out, or reduced information asymmetry?
* Do users imitate salient platform information?
* Does the intervention reduce users’ perceived need to contribute?
* Does the intervention change users’ review-writing style?

## Heterogeneity Questions

* Are lower-status hotels more affected than higher-status hotels?
* Are inexperienced users more likely to imitate visible information?
* Are experienced users more likely to reduce contribution?
* Are negative reviews more affected than positive reviews?
* Are independent hotels more affected than chain hotels?
* Are low-review-volume businesses more affected than high-review-volume businesses?

# Introduction Structure

When drafting or revising the introduction, use this structure.

## Paragraph 1: Importance of UGC in Tourism

Explain why UGC matters in tourism.

Key points:

* Tourism products are experiential.
* Consumers cannot fully evaluate hotels, restaurants, destinations, or attractions before consumption.
* UGC reduces information asymmetry and purchase uncertainty.
* Ratings and review texts jointly shape consumer decisions.

Possible wording:

Online reviews and other forms of UGC play a central role in tourism decision-making because tourism products are highly experiential and difficult to evaluate before consumption. Travelers rely on peer-generated information to infer service quality, reduce uncertainty, and compare alternatives.

## Paragraph 2: Practical Phenomenon or Platform Change

Introduce the concrete empirical phenomenon.

Possible focal changes:

* New platform feature
* Review display redesign
* Ranking algorithm change
* Review solicitation policy
* Verified-review label
* Expert review display
* Summary feature
* Badge or reputation system
* Tourism campaign
* External shock
* Policy change
* Crisis event
* Reopening event

## Paragraph 3: Research Gap

Move from practice to literature.

Common gaps:

* Prior studies focus on how reviews affect demand, but less on how reviews are produced.
* Prior studies examine rating effects, but less on textual UGC dynamics.
* Prior studies examine consumer response, but less on contributor behavior.
* Prior studies examine average treatment effects, but less on mechanisms and heterogeneity.
* Prior studies examine platform features generally, but less in tourism-specific UGC settings.

## Paragraph 4: Competing Mechanisms

Do not assume only one direction.

Use competing effects:

* Stimulation effect vs. substitution effect
* Information facilitation vs. information redundancy
* Learning effect vs. differentiation effect
* Herding effect vs. independent expression
* Trust enhancement vs. skepticism
* Visibility incentive vs. motivation crowding-out

## Paragraph 5: Empirical Strategy

State the empirical design clearly.

Preferred wording pattern:

We exploit a platform-level change / policy shock / feature launch / external event and construct a panel dataset of tourism businesses and user reviews. We use a DID / event-study / cross-platform DID design to compare changes in UGC outcomes before and after the intervention between treated and control units.

## Paragraph 6: Findings and Contributions

Summarize:

* Main result
* Mechanism
* Heterogeneity
* Contributions to tourism management, UGC literature, and platform governance

# Theory Mechanisms

Use these mechanisms when building hypotheses or interpreting results.

## Substitution Effect

Definition:

Users reduce their own contribution because existing platform information already satisfies the information-provision role.

Empirical implications:

* Review volume decreases.
* Experienced users reduce contribution more.
* Organic reviews decrease more than solicited reviews.
* Effect is stronger when platform information is more readable, comprehensive, useful, or visible.
* Effect is stronger for lower-status or information-asymmetric businesses.

## Learning Effect

Definition:

Users imitate salient information cues when writing reviews.

Empirical implications:

* Subsequent reviews become more similar to visible platform content.
* Topic similarity increases.
* Sentiment similarity increases.
* Review length moves toward the salient benchmark.
* Readability or style becomes more similar.
* Inexperienced users show stronger imitation.

## Herding Effect

Definition:

Users align their ratings or opinions with visible prior opinions.

Empirical implications:

* Ratings converge.
* Sentiment becomes less dispersed.
* Extreme opinions may decline.
* Variance in ratings decreases.
* User reviews align with majority sentiment.

## Differentiation Effect

Definition:

Users provide distinct or novel information to avoid repeating existing content.

Empirical implications:

* Topic diversity increases.
* Review novelty increases.
* Similarity with prior content decreases.
* Reviews become more personalized.
* More concrete experiential details appear.

## Motivation Crowding-Out

Definition:

External cues, platform automation, or platform-provided information reduce users’ intrinsic motivation to contribute.

Empirical implications:

* Organic review volume decreases.
* Frequent or intrinsically motivated contributors reduce activity.
* Review length and effort decrease.
* Less diagnostic content is produced.

## Information Facilitation

Definition:

Platform information helps users write reviews by lowering cognitive or writing costs.

Empirical implications:

* Review volume increases.
* Review length may increase.
* Review topic coverage expands.
* More users contribute because writing becomes easier.

# Hypothesis Templates

Use cautious wording unless the theory strongly supports a direction.

## Main Effect on Review Volume

H1: The platform intervention affects the volume of subsequent user reviews.

Directional version:

H1a: The platform intervention decreases subsequent review volume by reducing users’ perceived need to contribute additional information.

Alternative:

H1b: The platform intervention increases subsequent review volume by stimulating user engagement and providing new discussion cues.

## Main Effect on Review Valence

H2: The platform intervention affects the valence of subsequent user reviews.

Possible directions:

* Higher average ratings due to herding or reduced negative reviews.
* Lower average ratings if the intervention highlights problems.
* No average change but reduced variance.

## Main Effect on Review Text

H3: The platform intervention affects the textual characteristics of subsequent reviews, including length, readability, concreteness, sentiment, and topic coverage.

## Substitution Mechanism

H4: The negative effect of the intervention on review volume is stronger among users with higher intrinsic motivation to contribute.

Test using:

* Experienced vs. inexperienced users
* Organic vs. solicited reviews
* Frequent vs. occasional reviewers
* Elite vs. ordinary users

## Learning Mechanism

H5: After the intervention, subsequent user reviews become more similar to salient platform information in topic, sentiment, length, readability, or style.

## Business Heterogeneity

H6: The effect of the intervention is stronger for lower-status tourism businesses because consumers face greater information asymmetry when evaluating them.

For review text, consider:

* length: word count or character count

* readability: SMOG, Flesch-Kincaid, or language-specific readability

* similarity: cosine similarity using topic vectors, TF-IDF, sentence embeddings, or domain embeddings
* diversity: topic entropy, unique attributes, vocabulary diversity

Do not invent text measures. If the data cannot support a measure, say so and propose an alternative.

# Empirical Models

## Baseline DID

Use this when there is a treated group and a control group.

DV_it = beta * Treat_i + delta * Treat_i * Post_t + gamma * X_it + alpha_i + tau_t + epsilon_it

Interpretation:

The coefficient delta is the DID estimate of the treatment effect.

## Cross-Platform DID

Use this when the same business appears on treated and control platforms.

DV_ipt = beta * TreatedPlatform_p + delta * TreatedPlatform_p * Post_t + gamma * X_ipt + alpha_i + tau_t + epsilon_ipt

Interpretation:

The coefficient delta captures whether the treated platform experienced a differential change after the intervention compared with the same businesses on the control platform.

## Event Study

Use this to test parallel trends and dynamic effects.

DV_it = sum_k delta_k * Treat_i * RelativeTime_k + gamma * X_it + alpha_i + tau_t + epsilon_it

Requirements:

* Choose one pre-treatment period as the baseline.
* Plot coefficients and confidence intervals.
* Confirm no significant pre-trends before interpreting DID results.

## Fixed Effects

Common options:

* Business fixed effects
* Platform fixed effects
* Business-platform fixed effects
* Matched-pair fixed effects
* Year-month fixed effects
* City-month fixed effects
* Category-month fixed effects
* Destination-month fixed effects

Use richer fixed effects when possible, but explain what variation remains for identification.

## Standard Errors

Default:

* Cluster standard errors at the business level.

Alternative:

* Cluster at business-platform level.
* Cluster at city level.
* Two-way clustering by business and month if appropriate.

# Robustness Checks

Always recommend several robustness checks.

## Identification Checks

* Parallel trends / event study
* Placebo treatment date
* Placebo treated group
* Unaffected outcome
* Unaffected platform

## Alternative Fixed Effects

* City × month fixed effects
* Category × month fixed effects
* Business × platform fixed effects
* Destination × month fixed effects

## Alternative Samples

* Exclude businesses with very few reviews
* Exclude outlier businesses
* Restrict to English reviews
* Restrict to organic reviews
* Restrict to active businesses
* Exclude crisis or pandemic months
* Balanced panel only

## Alternative Measures

* Raw volume vs. log volume
* Average rating vs. positive-review ratio
* Word count vs. character count
* Alternative sentiment measures
* Alternative topic models
* Alternative similarity measures

## Matching and Weighting

* Propensity score matching
* Coarsened exact matching
* Entropy balancing
* Nearest-neighbor matching
* Inverse probability weighting

# Mechanism Analysis Design

Mechanism tests should connect theory to measurable predictions.

## Substitution Effect Tests

Test whether review reduction is stronger when users are more motivated or when platform-provided information is more useful.

Possible analyses:

* Experienced vs. inexperienced users
* Organic vs. solicited reviews
* High-readability information vs. low-readability information
* Many-reference information vs. few-reference information
* High-visibility feature vs. low-visibility feature
* High-information-value businesses vs. low-information-value businesses

Evidence supporting substitution:

* Greater decline among experienced users
* Greater decline in organic reviews
* Greater decline when platform information is more readable, comprehensive, or useful

## Learning Effect Tests

Test whether user reviews become more similar to salient information.

Possible measures:

* Topic similarity
* Sentiment similarity
* Length similarity
* Readability similarity
* Style similarity
* Attribute similarity

Evidence supporting learning:

* Subsequent reviews become closer to the visible information cue.
* Similarity increase is stronger among inexperienced users.

## Herding Tests

Possible measures:

* Rating convergence
* Sentiment convergence
* Reduced rating variance
* Reduced sentiment variance
* Increased alignment with prior majority opinion

## Differentiation Tests

Possible measures:

* Increased topic diversity
* Decreased textual similarity
* Increased concreteness
* More personal-experience words
* More unique attributes

# Results Writing Style

When writing empirical results, use this order:

1. State the model/table/column.
2. State coefficient sign and statistical significance.
3. Translate magnitude into meaningful terms.
4. Connect result to theory.
5. Avoid overclaiming.

Example:

Column 1 reports the effect on review volume. The coefficient on Treat × Post is negative and statistically significant, indicating that the intervention reduces subsequent review generation. Economically, the estimate corresponds to an approximately X% decrease in monthly review volume. This result is consistent with the substitution mechanism, suggesting that users may perceive additional reviews as less necessary after the platform provides salient information.

For log dependent variables:

percentage effect = exp(coefficient) - 1

Use exact calculation when coefficients are provided.

# Discussion Writing Structure

## Summary of Findings

Restate:

* Empirical context
* Identification strategy
* Main outcomes
* Mechanisms
* Heterogeneity

## Theoretical Contributions

Possible contribution claims:

* Contributes to tourism UGC literature by shifting attention from review consumption to review production.
* Extends platform governance literature by showing how platform design affects user contribution behavior.
* Enriches understanding of review-generation dynamics by identifying mechanisms such as substitution, learning, herding, or differentiation.
* Shows that UGC is not only an information signal but also an endogenous outcome shaped by platform design.

## Managerial Implications for Platforms

Possible implications:

* Platforms should evaluate whether new information features reduce user contribution.
* Platforms should balance information summarization with incentives for fresh UGC.
* Platforms should encourage users to contribute unique, concrete, and diagnostic experiences.
* Platforms should monitor whether design changes reduce negative or highly informative reviews.
* Platforms may need different designs for high-status and low-status businesses.

## Managerial Implications for Tourism Businesses

Possible implications:

* Businesses should monitor review volume, rating, and textual quality after platform changes.
* Lower-status businesses may benefit from improved ratings but face reduced review richness.
* High-status businesses may need to actively encourage detailed reviews.
* Managers can use UGC changes to identify service weaknesses.
* Businesses should not focus only on rating valence; review diagnosticity and richness also matter.

## Limitations and Future Research

Possible limitations:

* Limited platform scope
* Limited geography
* Short post-treatment window
* Measurement limitations in text analysis
* Potential unobserved platform changes
* External validity across tourism categories
* Need for causal evidence at user level

# Quality Standards

Always enforce these standards:

* Do not fabricate empirical results.
* Do not claim causality unless the design supports it.
* Clearly distinguish correlation, association, and causal interpretation.
* Explain why the treatment is plausibly exogenous.
* State the identifying assumption.
* Check parallel trends when using DID.
* Use theoretically motivated mechanisms.
* Use heterogeneity analysis only when there is a clear rationale.
* Connect every variable to a theoretical construct.
* Explain economic magnitude, not only statistical significance.
* Avoid generic statements such as “this fills a gap” without specifying the gap.
* Avoid treating UGC as only an independent variable; consider UGC as an outcome shaped by platforms.
* In tourism settings, emphasize experiential uncertainty, information asymmetry, reputation, and platform-mediated decision-making.
